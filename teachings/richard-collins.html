Richard Reishin Collins

A published poet, translator, and former university dean, Richard Collins began Zen practice with Robert Livingston in 2001, received monastic ordination in 2010, and permission to teach in 2012. When Livingston Roshi retired in 2016, Collins received shiho — Dharma transmission — and became the Temple’s second Abbot. He now resides at Stone Nest Dojo, the practice base he founded in Sewanee, Tennessee, and returns to New Orleans periodically to teach. Alongside his teaching, he continues a long second career as a writer: essays, poetry, and translations that carry Deshimaru’s and Livingston’s teaching into new form.

Selected Essays

Collins writes an ongoing series of essays and reflections — dharma commentary, literary criticism, and personal memoir, rooted in decades of practice in the lineage of Kodo Sawaki and Taisen Deshimaru. A few recurring pieces:

The Stink of Zen and the Return to Nature

Zazen does not need a formal ritual space but only a space at the source of one’s own nature.

In “Stink of Zen, Poison Dragons” (Tricycle, Summer 2026), Collins takes up perhaps his most persistent theme — the tension between institutional religion and direct experience — distinguishing the fragrance-accumulating temple from the source one finds by periodically leaving it behind.

Delusion, Dreams, and the Present Moment

Delusions are endless; the vow is not to eliminate delusion but to keep turning toward clarity, moment by moment.

Collins returns often to Deshimaru’s teaching that “delusion itself is satori.” A New Year’s essay reflects on the joya no kane ceremony, where the bell rings 108 times to dispel the 108 bonnos, alongside the Four Great Vows.

The Teacher and the Transmission

A disciple who left for five years and returned to eventually receive transmission.

Robert Livingston Roshi appears throughout Collins’s writing as a presiding spirit — mourned, dreamed of, carried. His own relationship to Livingston animates much of his writing on authority, autonomy, and what it means to inherit a practice.

Poetry and the Ancient Chinese Poets

Mountain solitude as practice ground.

Collins’s literary background gives his writing unusual density and range. His Stone Nest collection (Shanti Arts, 2025) places him in conversation with Wang Wei, Han Shan, and other poets who chose the mountains as their practice ground — a fitting echo of Stone Nest Dojo itself.

Community Practice and the Sitting Life

Alongside the more literary essays, Collins documents the rhythm of collective practice: seasonal zazenkai, retreat announcements, and reflections on why sitting together differs from solitary practice.
