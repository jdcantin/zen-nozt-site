The Masters

“From the time of Buddha to that of Bodhidharma, seven hundred years went by, from Bodhidharma to Dogen another seven hundred years; and from Dogen to Deshimaru seven hundred years.”

Kodo Sawaki Roshi (1880–1965)

Orphaned as a child and raised by a brutal uncle, Kodo Sawaki worked as a boy in a brothel and at gambling tables before being sent, at fourteen, to the front of the Sino-Japanese War. Wounded and left for dead under a pile of corpses for days, he survived and walked, at sixteen, four days and nights to the Temple of Eiheiji, where the monks at first refused to let in a ragged, half-mute stranger. He stayed. He never took charge of a monastery or settled in a temple, remaining to the end an unsui — a wandering monk — who brought zazen to cities, fishing villages, universities and prisons alike. In 1965, on his deathbed, he passed the Transmission and his kesa to his disciple Taisen Deshimaru.

Taisen Deshimaru Roshi (1914–1982)

Born to an old samurai family in Kyushu, Deshimaru studied Christianity and Rinzai Zen before finding his teacher, Kodo Sawaki, and becoming his disciple on the spot. Sent to the Pacific front in WWII, he survived the sinking of two ships — once sitting zazen on a case of dynamite as torpedoes struck his convoy, once thrown into the sea and found afloat a day and night later, his master’s rakusu still intact. Imprisoned as a resistance sympathizer and freed just before a scheduled execution, he later rejoined Sawaki and received the Transmission at his master’s deathbed in 1965. After burying Sawaki’s ashes, Deshimaru sat unmoving for forty-nine days, then left Japan for Paris, where he taught for the rest of his life — the disciple who, as Sawaki had asked him, carried true Zen to the West.

Robert Livingston Roshi (1933–2021)

Born in New York, Livingston served in the U.S. Army in Japan and Korea, then spent a decade in the financial industry in Europe before retiring to practice Zen with Deshimaru in Paris. He became a close disciple and was ordained a teacher. Before his death in 1982, Deshimaru asked Livingston to go to America and open a dojo. Livingston founded the American Zen Association and the New Orleans Zen Temple in 1983, teaching in New Orleans for nearly forty years until his death in early 2021.

Richard Reishin Collins — Abbot since 2016

Born in Eugene, Oregon in 1952, Richard Collins came to Zen practice relatively late — a published poet, translator, and former university dean who began sitting with Robert Livingston in 2001. He received monastic ordination in 2010 and permission to teach in 2012. When Livingston Roshi retired in 2016, Collins received shiho (Dharma transmission) and became the Temple’s second Abbot, continuing the line from Sawaki through Deshimaru and Livingston. He now resides at Stone Nest Dojo in Sewanee, Tennessee, returning to New Orleans periodically to teach, and continues to write and translate — his essays on practice, poetry, and the teachers who shaped him form an ongoing companion to the older teishos gathered on this site.
